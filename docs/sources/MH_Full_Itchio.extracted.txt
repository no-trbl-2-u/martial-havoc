Introduction 
Martial Havoc is a rule-light, d6 (six-sided dice) only, roleplaying game that 
allows you to easily create a character and start playing in just a few 
minutes;  it stems from a love for the WuXia genre and traditional Chinese 
martial arts, inspired by films such as "Five Fingers of Violence”, "Drunken 
Master” or "Crouching Tiger, Hidden Dragon”. 
You will play endless fights, flying kicks, close-ups of furrowed brows, 
sharp blades grabbed with bare hands, demons with magical powers, and 
the hero (the Master) seeking revenge, fortune or fame.  
WuXia is generally understood to refer to both "kung fu" stories, without 
supernatural elements and the XianXia genre, where spirits and humans 
coexist in a European fantasy-like world. Choose the path you prefer, or 
mix the two subgenres, what matters is that you have fun. 
Some terms or mechanics are intentionally ambiguous to encourage player 
interpretation. The rulebook gives you the tools, the story comes from your 
imagination. 
This game is a hack of Heroic Havoc - 
ruolatorisolitari.itch.io/heroic-havoc

● Common clothing 
● A weapon (even if not listed in the weapon table pg. 53)

Attributes 
Determine SKILL, ENDURANCE, and LUCK points as follows: 
● SKILL: Roll 1d6 and add 6 to the result. 
● ENDURANCE: Roll 2d6 and add 12 to the result. 
● LUCK: Roll 1d6 and add 6 to the result. 
These initial stats are crucial. Although they may fluctuate, they usually 
only increase above their initial  values in exceptional circumstances.  
● SKILL represents combat prowess, dexterity, and agility. 
● ENDURANCE reflects your health and physical condition; if it 
reaches zero, the Master dies or becomes unconscious. 
● LUCK indicates your good fortune.

Training 
The Training skill allows you to learn Techniques and Rituals. 
The difference between them is in the activation time: 
Techniques are immediate and can also be used in combat, 
while Rituals require preparation time, such as invoking a 
deity, meditation, or physical exercise. 
To obtain the Training skill, during character creation, 
subtract points from the SKILL attribute (1 SKILL point = 1 
Training point). 
The total SKILL attribute will thus be permanently 
reduced, but without affecting the total points to be spent 
during character creation for your Martial Proficiencies. 
For each Training point spent, you gain 4 points to spend on learning 
Techniques and Rituals from the tables below. 
In the game, use Training skill as one of your Martial Proficiency, but keep 
in mind that performing these movements or practices requires mental 
and physical effort, so you lose as many ENDURANCE points as indicated 
(from 1 to 4). 
E.g. You  roll SKILL=9 at creation for a Master in  Improvised Weapons deciding to 
assign 2 points to the Training skill. You have now 8 Resource points to spend in 
Technics and Rituals; 9 SKILL points to assign in  Armed combat, Throwing 
weapons or Surprise attack (Martial Art Proficiencies); but your final SKILL points 
will be 7.

Actions 
There are two ways to determine the success of an action: 
● SKILL check: When you have to decide the outcome of an action 
that depends directly on the Master's skills. The threshold is equal 
to SKILL + Martial Proficiency points (if relevant). 
 
● LUCK check: When you have to decide the outcome of an action 
that does not depend on the Master's skills, but on external 
factors.  
In both cases, roll 2d6 and compare the result with the relevant attribute. If 
the result is equal to or lower, the check is successful. Otherwise, it is a 
failure. 
After making a LUCK check, subtract one point from the total LUCK value, 
regardless of the outcome. If you succeed, the check is successful. 
Otherwise, you have been unlucky. 
 
22 

 
Combat 
To resolve a combat, calculate the Attack Strength for both you and your 
opponent: 
Roll 2d6, add the result to the value of one relevant Martial Proficiency (if 
any) and to your SKILL points, the result is your total Attack Strength 
(=2d6+Martial Proficiency+SKILL) 
If your opponent has the higher Attack Strength, they damage you, 
subtracting the difference between the two Attack Strengths from your 
ENDURANCE. 
If your Attack Strength is higher  than your opponent, you can do one of 
the following: 
○ Subtract the difference in Attack Strengths from 
your opponent’s ENDURANCE. 
○ Use one of the Techniques you know 
○ Change or recover a weapon 
○ Create an Opening 
The combat continues until: 
○ You succeed in landing a Final Blow  
○ Your opponent's or your ENDURANCE points reach zero 
○ An Unexpected Event occurs 
23 

 
Use a technique 
You can use one of the Techniques you know without making a roll check, 
but you must subtract as many ENDURANCE points as the value of the 
Technique. 
In times of need, the Master can concentrate internal energy and perform 
a spectacular action without failing.  
To make the fights more challenging, do not use the Technique to end a 
fight; even if you can knock down an opponent, it does not mean that they 
are defeated, but that you have gained a great advantage, or the chance to 
escape without consequences. 
Creating an Opening 
You do not damage your opponent, but your last attack has exposed a 
weakness in your opponent or made them harmless. You can take 
advantage to deliver a Final Blow.  
24 

 
Final Blow 
After creating an opening, roll 2d6. If both dice show the same number, 
your blow lands, giving you the chance to deliver a devastating strike. 
To add  this new Technique to your Martial Arts knowledge, roll against 
your current LUCK. On a failure, lose 1 LUCK; on success assign it a value 
(1–4) with a brief description. For inspiration, roll  2d6 on the following 
table, the attributes suit both the action and the animal. 
E.g. Destroying Palm of the Turtle / Palm of the Destroyer  Turtle / Deadly Palm of 
the Destroyer Turtle 
E.g. New technique: Impetuous Slap of the Phoenix (2). I jump and strike my 
opponent's cheek, leaving a red scar on their face.  
E.g. New technique:  Spiritual Headbutt of the Unicorn (4). I project an energy 
wave towards my opponent, who loses memory of the fight. 
E.g. New technique: Fox's Spinning Jump (1):  a sudden kick and I find myself in 
the crowd, escaping the fight

Unexpected event 
When the final result of an attack roll ends in a draw (you and your 
opponent have the same Attack Strength), an Unexpected Event occurs. It 
does not necessarily have to be an extreme incident, but can also be a 
simple diversion. 
 After an Unexpected Event, you are no longer in the combat phase. 
Imagine the two opponents still in the attack position, crossing their 
weapons and staring into each other's eyes for seconds... then something 
happens. If it is not clear from the ongoing narrative, roll on the following 
table:

Multiple Combat 
Reduce your SKILL points by an amount equal to the 
number of opponents you face.Remember that some 
Techniques or Martial Proficiencies can help you in multiple 
combat: in case of a successful attack, distribute the same 
amount of damage to the enemies.  
E.g. you have the Martial Proficiency Double Strike, your attack 
against 3 opponents causes 4 damage; two of them will suffer 4 
damage. 
The opponent’s ATTACK attribute (see Opponents pg. 70) 
indicates how many enemies can attack at the same time. 
Escape 
During a fight, if you think the enemy is too tough, you can 
flee. If you have not used a Technique or some other 
stratagem for a daring escape, suffer a last blow and 
subtract 2  points from your ENDURANCE.  
Score 1 Dishonor Point for each time you fail to escape 
without suffering damage. At the end of the adventure, your 
Dishonor Points will affect your total Experience Points (see 
Experience and Advancement pg. 34).

Healing 
 
● SKILL: this attribute isn’t usually affected, but if it is, you can 
restore part of it (1 point) with spiritual regeneration Techniques. 
It recovers completely after a full night's rest. 
 
 
● ENDURANCE: partially recovers (4 points) with healing 
techniques, spiritual regeneration, eating a meal, or with a Health 
Elixir. It recovers completely with a week’s rest. 
 
 
● LUCK: partially recovered (1 point) with a Spirituality roll check in 
a Temple (see Exploring the City pg. 47)

Experience and Advancement 
At the end of each adventure, assign a number from 1 (poor) to 3 (excellent) 
for each of the following: 
● Mission Success 
● Use of equipment and environment 
● Combat spectacularity 
● Lateral thinking 
Add the four values together and subtract your Dishonor Points (see 
Escape pg. 30) from the total to obtain the XP you can spend on 
advancement. A Master with low SKILL points will have more 
opportunities to improve their martial Proficiencies; a Master with a high 
SKILL level will have almost reached perfection in their style and will be 
able to expand their knowledge in other areas. 
Use this table to determine the XP needed to increase the Master's 
attributes by 1 point:

SKILL 8 XP 10 XP 12 XP 
ENDURANCE 4 XP 4 XP 4 XP

SKILL and LUCK attributes cannot exceed 12 points, while Martial 
Proficiency can exceed the initial maximum value of 4. 
To learn new  Techniques or Rituals, you will need to increase your 
Training Skill (by spending XP). 
E.g. Master Lee:  SKILL=11 LUCK=9 ENDURANCE=24 
In saving the governor's son from the  bandits, Master Lee obtained the following: 
Mission success: 2  
Use of equipment and environment: 3  
Combat spectacularity: 1  
Lateral thinking: 3 
Dishonor points: 1 
Total= 8 
With 8 XP, I can choose to increase ENDURANCE by 1 or 2 points (4 XP) or my 
LUCK by 1 point (6 XP); or I can increase my Training skill  by 1 point (6 XP), 
obtaining 4 Resource Points to spend on learning new Techniques or Rituals. 
Any remaining XP will remain available to spend on the next advancement.

Exploration 
Follow these steps to explore the world: 
1. Establish a starting city and determine its Resources by rolling on 
the Region Exploration table. 
2. Follow the Region Exploration procedure and determine the 
surrounding locations (consider the starting city in the center). 
3. If you decide to travel and explore, roll to determine the 
characteristics of the locations you want to visit. 
4. When you move to a location or start a new scene, roll for an Event 
to determine what happens. 
5. If the Event involves an encounter, roll on the Oracle’s Encounter 
Outcome row pg. 58. If the result is Attack or Ambush, start a 
combat; otherwise, roll on the NPC or Creature Reaction row. 
6. If you find a Monastery, you may decide to enter and explore it. 
Follow the Monastery generation procedure. 
7. When in doubt, use the Oracle tools to determine the answers you 
would normally ask the Game Master.

Explore the cities 
The city you are staying in is not just a shelter, but a living place where your 
adventures can begin and develop.  
Explore some of the city's locations to interact with people who may help, 
or hinder, your plans.  
 
Gambling House 
Here you can play Fantan or Sic Bo and hope to earn a few coins; but be 
careful, if you are too lucky, they will make it clear that it is time to go 
home. 
To settle a bet, decide on the amount, then you and the Dealer (see 
Opponents pg.  71) roll 2d6: use your current LUCK points, adding any 
points from a Martial Proficiency that may help you, and the Dealer uses 
their SKILL points. Whoever gets the highest score wins the bet. 
 If you don't have the money to pay, a fight begins. You do not have to 
decrease your LUCK points when rolling for a bet. 
46 

 
Temple 
Whether it's a large complex or a small altar among the shacks, it doesn't 
matter, here you will always find flowers, people using divination tools 
such as Jiaobei, and a cloud of smoke rising from an incense burner. 
If you have incense, you can attempt a Spirituality check (SKILL check plus 
any Martial Proficiency or Technique that may help you) at the three stars 
of good fortune - Sanxing. If successful, you recover 1 LUCK point. 
Abusing the patience of the gods could cause the opposite effect, or worse, 
bring a curse upon yourself. 
 
Central District 
Administrative and residential center for magistrates, mandarins, and 
nobles. Luxurious Siheyuan, courtyard houses, surrounded by neat 
avenues. 
The area is protected by walls, with guards at the four cardinal entrances 
and patrols with lanterns on the streets at night. 
Entering here armed or dressed in common clothes will not be easy. 
47 

 
Market 
Not a square, but a series of streets divided by type of merchandise in an 
orderly chaos of shouting, food, animals, beggars, street performers, 
puppeteers, scammers... one wrong move can create chaos with 
unpredictable consequences. 
You can find everything you're looking for, but don't expect to resell your 
goods at full price unless you're a skilled negotiator. 
City Walls 
To respect cosmic symmetry, a large city should have three entrances on 
each side, a medium city one entrance on each side, and a small one 
entrance on the south side. 
Each entrance is controlled, closed at night by iron gates and surmounted 
by a tower. The walls are walkable and made of brick. 
Guards stamp passports on entry and exit; passing quickly or unseen is 
very difficult. 
 
 
 
 
48 

 
Hutong 
A maze of narrow streets separates the more modest courtyard houses of 
ordinary citizens: merchants, artisans, professionals. Here everyone 
returns to rest after a day's work, to enjoy the silence of the night. 
It is easy to get lost, but also to cover your tracks. If the streets seem quiet, 
look up and keep an eye on the rooftops. 
 
Chaguan 
The tea room, a place to relax and socialize. Over a cup of green tea or a 
chess game, people discuss business, job offers, and resolve disputes. 
The presence of a small stage attracts artists for theater performances, 
storytellers, and acrobats. 
If you want to show off, you can play Chinese chess or Go: 
Make a Concentration check (SKILL check plus any Proficiency or 
Technique/Ritual that may help you). If you pass the check, roll 2d6. With a 
double result, you have demonstrated your superiority and won the game. 
If you fail the Concentration check, reduce your ENDURANCE by 1; it's an 
exhausting game. Continue until you win or abandon the game.

Encounters 
To randomly determine an encounter, use the following table, rolling on 
the relevant column. 
If you need to roll for a Martial Art, the value in parentheses represents 
how many points each Martial Proficiency has. 
If you need to roll for s Technique or Ritual, add the value in parentheses to 
the opponent's Attack Strength. Your opponent does not spend 
ENDURANCE points to perform Techniques or Rituals. 
Sometimes you will face spirits or ghosts, incorporeal beings immune to 
traditional weapons or blows; you will need to use a technique, ritual, or 
exceptional weapon to defeat them.

Appendix A: Philosophy of the open system 
This game is based on the philosophy of a game in continuous 
development. Anyone can contribute to changing the rules, expanding 
parts of the world, or adding details.

  Appendix B: Martial Havoc - Cinematic 
Journey  
It is possible to experience Solo RPG as a sandbox experience in which the 
protagonist lives endless adventures, between tables and dice rolls, until 
the demiurge decides to change the game. 
Martial Havoc - Cinematic Journey (hereinafter referred to as MH-CJ) is an 
alternative tool for creating stories and bringing them to fruition in 90 
minutes of imaginary television time; the three-act theory is used to 
harness the narrative into a predefined structure, accompanying the player 
in reliving simple adventures of revenge and magic seen on TV. 
81 

 
Three-act structure 
 
  This is a simplified outline of the three-act theory, adapted to the clichés of 
Hong Kong film productions. We see how the narrative tension, once past 
the initial phase, tends to rise continuously until it reaches the final 
climax. 
  The symbol  represents a plot point, an event in the story that has an 
impact on the characters or the world, and pushes it in a new direction. 
  Each act ends with a climax, a point of maximum tension; a point of no 
return to a new act. The protagonist cannot physically go back, or events 
have changed him irreversibly. 
82 

 
  Obstacles represent the engine that drives the story forward: let's imagine 
them as a circular movement that starts with the presentation of the 
obstacle and ends with its resolution. In the end, we will be back on the 
same narrative line, ready to reach the next step. This type of simple 
Obstacle can become complicated, giving rise to sub-Obstacles that must 
be resolved before completing the initial Obstacle.            
  It is important not to open too many sub-obstacles and to return to the 
narrative path to maintain tension towards the objective. 
                                                                               
  In MH-CJ, consider most sub-obstacles as fights, i.e., the means to 
continue resolving the main obstacle: entering a building, escaping from a 
building, convincing someone, etc. 
   
83 

 
  Although in a film it is possible to decide the number of Obstacles, in Solo 
RPGs, and more generally in emerging fiction, we do not know how many 
trials our protagonist will have to overcome, but we can interpret the 
results of the dice rolls to understand if we have reached a plot point.  
  If the result of the dice roll conflicts with the linear development of the 
story, ignore the dice. Reach the plot point without lowering the tension. 
  For example, at the beginning of the third act, the protagonist has defeated 
the generals guarding the room where the boss resides and is opening the 
door; a roll on the Event table with Rest result, although plausible, would 
slow down the momentum. Instead, let the Encounter happen and prepare 
for the finale. 
   
  Finally, in order not to lose sight of the Objective and stay on track with the 
plot, you can draw the outline as you play, marking the transition to a new 
act and the achievement of plot points. 
  Act I - Presentation 
Define the world where the story takes place with its rules (realism, 
presence of the supernatural, historical time) and introduce the 
characters.  
Call/Incident: an event that changes the balance of the world and involves 
the protagonist. It defines the initial Goal. 
Motivation/Point of No Return: the push that convinces the protagonist to 
accept the call. 
Climax: the turning point of the first act, representing the abandonment 
of the Ordinary World just presented. The protagonist is ready for action 
and to enter a New World. 
84 

 
 
In Solo RPG, the protagonist and the world in which they live are defined 
by rolling on the creation tables and interpreting the results. Similarly, the 
Incident can be generated from the "Adventures" table pg. 36. 
 
The Motivation, if not obvious, can emerge by playing the first act: the 
protagonist lives in a world altered by the Incident and their life is 
irrevocably disrupted, until they accept the Call. 
 
As an additional creation tool, it is recommended to use Faser "Adventure 
Generator"  (published in Italian by Ruolatori Solitari: 
https://ruolatorisolitari.itch.io/faser). 
 
In MH-CJ, when defining the world, you decide whether magic is present. 
If so, go overboard with the special effects; otherwise, remember that a 
deity can manifest itself with flashes of fire or as an invisible hand. 
 
Also, set concrete goals, avoid politics and complicated romantic 
entanglements.  
  Act 2 - Confrontation 
We move into the Other World. It can be a physical place with different 
rules or a new social context. This is the most dynamic moment in the 
story; in order to achieve their Goal, the protagonist is forced to overcome 
a series of Obstacles. 
First part: The Obstacles help us learn more about the Other World and 
introduce new characters. 
85 

 
Central turning point - twist/revelation/progress: divides the second act 
into two parts; the Obstacles overcome so far clarify the goal by revealing 
unknown details. The antagonist may be revealed. The initial Goal may 
change.  
Second part: The obstacles become more dangerous, the antagonist more 
pressing, and the goal draws nearer. 
Crisis/failure/death: The goal recedes. Often the loss of a companion or a 
near-death experience shakes the protagonist's hope and the actual 
possibility of achieving the goal.  
Climax: The final goal becomes achievable again. Often, this involves a 
realization or "rebirth" of the protagonist. 
 
In MH-CJ, the second act represents almost the entirety of the narrative. 
The focus is on the challenges presented by the Obstacles, the discovery of 
the new world, and the interaction between the characters. 
 
There are two recurring narrative structures that alone can sustain the 
second act: climbing the pyramid, trial and error. 
 
Climbing the pyramid: on the way to the boss fight, the protagonist must 
first face a series of enemies starting from the base of an imaginary 
pyramid with waves of minions, then raise the level of the battle with fewer 
but more powerful enemies, until reaching the boss at the top of the 
pyramid.  
 
Trial and error: on the path to completing the mission, the protagonist is 
immediately confronted with the main challenge, which is impossible to 
86 

 
overcome. A sequence of improvements and trials will begin until he is 
ready (physically or mentally) to overcome the challenge.  
  Act 3 - Resolution 
This is the climax of the narrative tension, the confrontation with the 
antagonist or the conflict.  
Rising Action: the protagonist gathers their last strength to achieve the 
Goal. Obstacles may arise if not previously resolved, but usually no new 
challenges.  
Climax: in many cases, the boss fight, the protagonist clashes with the 
antagonist. Resolution of the main conflict. The Goal may or may not be 
achieved (success or failure). 
Resolution: the adventure is summed up, the protagonist is transformed 
by the challenges, and all the questions left open in the first two acts are 
answered. 
 
In Solo RPG, we decide whether to conclude the protagonist's narrative arc 
and end the game or to show how the protagonist and the world they live in 
have changed; level up and prepare for a new adventure.  
 
In MH-CJ, if you decide to end the adventure, you don't have to come up 
with a meaningful ending; a joyful jump and a freeze frame with the 
closing credits will suffice.    
 
If, on the other hand, you are ready to face new challenges with the same 
protagonist, pick up one of the narrative threads left unresolved in the 
87 

 
second act; or answer the question: which figure in the shadows was 
pulling the strings of the boss you just defeated? And start over.

The following are personal interpretations of the narrative structure of two 
films, without any claim to academic rigor. 
Clan of the White Lotus (1980) 
Act I  
In a country divided between the government and Shaolin rebels, the clash 
between two local fighters (Crane and Tiger) and the evil White Lotus Clan 
ends with the killing of Crane and Tiger's girlfriend. 
Climax: Tiger is forced to flee and hide in a safe place. 
 
Act II  
To get revenge, Tiger starts a series of training sessions, (failed) attacks on 
the White Lotus monastery, and escapes. He never manages to get to the 
head of the Clan. 
Twist: The protagonist realizes he must learn a new fighting method (the 
weaver's style), aimed at pressure points. 
Crisis: Yet another fight with the clan leader will see the protagonist close 
to death. 
Climax: Saved by acupuncture, the protagonist will have an intuition to 
face the boss again.

Act III 
Final battle with the leader of the White Lotus Clan 
Climax: Using sewing needles, the protagonist manages to strike the boss's 
fatal point. 
Resolution: Revenge is complete.

During the civil wars, a soldier deserts and enters a mysterious cave to
